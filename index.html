From business problem to a trustworthy baseline: framing the decision, auditing the data, splitting by time, and climbing the baseline ladder, told through SwiftBite's delivery promise.

Opening story

"Thirty minutes" is not a number

SwiftBite delivers food from about 400 restaurants across the city. When a customer places an order, the app shows a promise: "Arriving in 30 minutes."

Ishita, who runs operations, opens Monday's review with two slides. Slide 1: nearly one order in five arrives more than 10 minutes after the promise. Those customers complain, ask for refunds, and some never order again. Slide 2: when the team made promises longer to be safe, conversions fell. Customers saw "55 minutes" and ordered from a competitor.

"Our promise is a rule someone wrote years ago. Distance times a number, plus a few extras. I want a model that gives every order a promise we can trust."

Kabir, who joined the data team a month ago, is asked to build it. Dev, the product manager, adds one condition: "Show me it works before we ship, and show me it will keep working after."

Kabir's first instinct is to open a notebook and fit a model. His mentor stops him: "The model is about 20% of this job. What decision is the prediction for? What does a mistake cost? What do you actually know at the moment the customer taps 'order'? And how will you prove it works on days you have not seen yet?"

In Part 1, Kabir turns a business worry into a well-posed ML problem, audits the data, and builds a trustworthy baseline. Part 2 stress-tests it, improves it, and faces the real world.

Step 1

Frame the problem

Explorer · the framing questions

From cost to the right prediction

We are not asked to guess the average delivery time. We are asked to choose a promise p for each order. If the actual time is t:

cost = 3 · max(t − p, 0) + 1 · max(p − t, 0)

Raise the promise by one minute: every order that would have been late now saves 3 units, and every order that was already early now costs 1 more unit. Break-even is when the share of late orders is 1/4. So the best promise is the value that 75% of orders beat: the 75th percentile of the delivery-time distribution for that kind of order.

τ = clate / (clate + cearly) = 3 / (3 + 1) = 0.75
Interactive · how the cost ratio sets the target percentile
Best promise = this percentile
Share of orders that should be late

At a ratio of 1 the best promise is the median. As lateness gets costlier, the best promise moves up the distribution.

Why it matters. A model that predicts the average (squared error) aims at the wrong target. A model that predicts the 75th percentile (quantile or "pinball" loss) aims at the right one.

Metrics we report

On-time %

Share of orders delivered within the promise.

Late > 10 min %

The painful tail.

Average over-promise (min)

How much padding customers see.

Cost per order

The number above. Our primary metric, because it is the business decision in one number.

Code: setup and the cost function
import warnings; warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, matplotlib.pyplot as plt, seaborn as sns
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.preprocessing import OrdinalEncoder
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold

C_LATE, C_EARLY = 3.0, 1.0     # one minute late hurts 3x as much as one minute early

def promise_report(actual, promise):
    actual, promise = np.asarray(actual, float), np.asarray(promise, float)
    late = np.maximum(actual - promise, 0)      # minutes late (0 if on time)
    early = np.maximum(promise - actual, 0)     # minutes of over-promise (0 if late)
    return {"on_time_%": 100 * (actual <= promise).mean(),
            "late_>10min_%": 100 * (late > 10).mean(),
            "avg_over_promise_min": early.mean(),
            "cost_per_order": (C_LATE * late + C_EARLY * early).mean()}

promise_report([30, 40, 50], [35, 35, 35])
{'on_time_%': 33.33, 'late_>10min_%': 33.33, 'avg_over_promise_min': 1.67, 'cost_per_order': 21.67}

Step 5

Look at the data with a business lens

Plots on the training period (delivery time by hour, zone, distance and weather) lead to six hypotheses worth writing down.

Explorer · six hypotheses

Right-skewed target. A few very slow deliveries pull the mean above the median. The 75th percentile sits well above the mean. This is why a "predict the average" model will under-promise.

Schematic of the shape, not the plotted data.

Peak hours are slower, and the 75th percentile rises faster than the mean (more spread at peak).

Zones differ: dense or old areas are slower, suburbs are further but faster per km.

Weather matters, and its effect is larger in the tail.

Spread grows with distance: longer trips are slower and less predictable, so the same model must promise a wider margin for long trips. The right promise is order-specific.

Some orders have a recorded distance of 0 km or of 40 to 90 km but a normal delivery time. Those distances cannot be real. They were noted in the audit and are handled in Part 2.

Step 6

The baseline ladder

A model is only meaningful relative to simpler alternatives. We climb a ladder, and each rung must be beaten by the next.

The model's inputs

Two lists of columns are fixed: CAT (zone, restaurant type, weather) and NUM (hour, is_peak, restaurant_avg_prep_min, distance_km, n_items, order_value, temperature_c, traffic_index, riders_available_nearby). All are known at the moment the order is placed, and none of the leaky columns are included. The only fix applied is filling missing weather with "unknown", so "missing" becomes a category of its own. There is no scaling, number imputation or hyperparameter tuning yet.

One function that builds the model

make_model returns a pipeline with two steps. An OrdinalEncoder encodes categories, learning them from training data only (an unseen category becomes "missing" instead of crashing). Then HistGradientBoostingRegressor uses the loss we choose: loss="quantile", quantile=0.75 aims at the 75th percentile, while loss="squared_error" aims at the average.

Code: inputs, model and scoreboard
CAT = ["zone", "restaurant_type", "weather"]
NUM = ["hour", "is_peak", "restaurant_avg_prep_min", "distance_km", "n_items", "order_value",
       "temperature_c", "traffic_index", "riders_available_nearby"]

def get_X(frame, num=NUM, cat=CAT):
    X = frame[cat + num].copy()
    if "weather" in cat:
        X["weather"] = X["weather"].fillna("unknown")
    return X

def make_model(loss="quantile", quantile=0.75, cat=CAT, **params):
    pre = ColumnTransformer([("cat", OrdinalEncoder(handle_unknown="use_encoded_value", unknown_value=np.nan), cat)],
                            remainder="passthrough")
    hgb = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss=loss, quantile=quantile if loss == "quantile" else None,
                                        categorical_features=list(range(len(cat))), random_state=0, **params)
    return Pipeline([("prep", pre), ("model", hgb)])

LEDGER = []
def record(name, actual, promise):
    LEDGER.append({"model": name, **promise_report(actual, promise)})
    return pd.DataFrame(LEDGER).set_index("model").round(2)

record("1. current promise (rule)", y_va, val.promised_eta_min.values)
record("2. one number for all: 75th pct", y_va, np.full(len(val), np.quantile(y_tr, 0.75)))
# 3: 75th percentile by zone x peak (from train)
m_sq = make_model("squared_error").fit(get_X(train), y_tr)       # rungs 4 and 5
m_q  = make_model("quantile", 0.75).fit(get_X(train), y_tr)      # rung 6

Step 7

The too-good-to-be-true trap

A colleague suggests adding every column in the table. Let's see what happens when the post-delivery columns trip_min, pickup_wait_min and customer_rating are added.

Interactive · add leaky columns, watch the "improvement"

Code
LEAKY = ["trip_min", "pickup_wait_min", "customer_rating"]
for extra in [[], ["trip_min"], ["customer_rating"], LEAKY]:
    mdl = make_model("quantile", 0.75).fit(get_X(train, NUM).assign(**{c: train[c] for c in extra}), y_tr)
    pred = mdl.predict(get_X(val, NUM).assign(**{c: val[c] for c in extra}))

trip_min

Time from pickup to the doorstep: part of the answer.

customer_rating

Falls when the delivery was late, so it carries the outcome.

pickup_wait_min

Happens after the order is placed.

None of them exist when the promise has to be made. In production those columns would be empty, the model would fail, and the "improvement" would vanish. The gap between the honest and the leaky model is the leak.

Step 8

Features that are safe in production

A strong, legitimate signal is how each restaurant has behaved in the past: its average and variability in delivery time. It must use only earlier orders, as in production.

Interactive · shift(1) + expanding() (illustrative numbers)

Delivery times (min) for one restaurant, in order. Pick the order being predicted:

Illustrative values with a minimum of 3 past orders for the demo. The real feature uses min_periods=10. The order never sees its own delivery time or anything after it.

  • shift(1) pushes every value down one row, so the order never sees its own delivery time.
  • expanding() then averages everything before that point.
  • min_periods=10 returns "missing" until the restaurant has 10 past orders.
  • Three features result: past mean, past spread (std) and number of past orders. rest_hist_n is deliberately not used as a model input.
Code: add_history and rung 7
def add_history(frame_all, min_orders=10):
    d = frame_all.sort_values("order_time")
    g = d.groupby("restaurant_id").actual_delivery_min
    hist = pd.DataFrame({
        "rest_hist_mean": g.transform(lambda s: s.shift(1).expanding(min_periods=min_orders).mean()),
        "rest_hist_std":  g.transform(lambda s: s.shift(1).expanding(min_periods=min_orders).std()),
        "rest_hist_n":    g.cumcount(),
    })
    return frame_all.join(hist)

df = add_history(df)
train, val, test = df[df.month <= 4].copy(), df[df.month == 5].copy(), df[df.month == 6].copy()
NUM_V2 = NUM + ["rest_hist_mean", "rest_hist_std"]

m_h = make_model("quantile", 0.75).fit(get_X(train, NUM_V2), y_tr)
p_h = m_h.predict(get_X(val, NUM_V2))

What is the model actually using?

Permutation importance shuffles one column at a time on validation data and measures how much the business cost gets worse. The bigger the increase, the more the model relies on that column. We score by cost (not accuracy) because cost is the real metric. The top drivers are distance, rider supply, restaurant behaviour, traffic and weather, matching what the exploration showed.

Code
from sklearn.inspection import permutation_importance
sub = val.sample(3000, random_state=0)
pi = permutation_importance(m_h, get_X(sub, NUM_V2), sub.actual_delivery_min, n_repeats=3, random_state=0,
        scoring=lambda est, X, y_: -promise_report(y_, est.predict(X))["cost_per_order"])
Two cautions for production. Restaurants with fewer than 10 past orders get a missing history (the model treats it as unknown, exactly the situation for a brand-new restaurant). And in a live system the history must be refreshed from completed orders, which arrive with a delay.

Step 9

Write down the expectation

Before the sealed June data is opened, we make a written prediction of how the model should perform, based on validation only. Part 2 compares it with reality.

Cost per order · lower is better

Inside the stable months, random and time-based estimates agree closely (8.61 vs 8.40), so the split protocol does not change the answer here. That is no reason to skip it. The difference appears when conditions change, which is what happens in June.

Code
kf = KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
oof = np.zeros(len(train))
for a, b in kf.split(train):
    mm = make_model("quantile", 0.75).fit(get_X(train.iloc[a], NUM_V2), y_tr[a])
    oof[b] = mm.predict(get_X(train.iloc[b], NUM_V2))
rand_cost = promise_report(y_tr, oof)["cost_per_order"]
time_cost = promise_report(y_va, p_h)["cost_per_order"]
